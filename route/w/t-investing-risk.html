👂 Thursday: listen first, then read周四：先听懂，再看文字

“Why Losses Hurt More” is a text you have not read yet.「为什么亏损更让人难受」：这一篇你还没读过。 Three steps: listen without the text, answer, say the main points. Then open the text to check and shadow it.三步：不看文字听，答题，说出要点；然后打开原文核对并跟读。

1 · Answer without looking不看文字答题

1 Why do most people turn down the coin-toss bet?

The odds are even, but a loss tends to feel more painful than an equal gain feels pleasant.胜负机会是均等的，但亏损的痛苦往往大于同等收益的快乐。

2 According to the article, why might some investors keep a poor investment?

The third paragraph says they refuse to sell because selling would make the loss feel real.第三段指出，他们不肯卖出，是因为卖出会让亏损显得真实。

2 · Say the main points说出要点

3 · Now look at the text再看原文

📄 Show the text显示原文

- Imagine that someone offers you a simple bet on a coin toss. If it lands heads, you win a certain sum; if it lands tails, you lose exactly the same amount. Most people turn it down, even though the odds are perfectly even. Psychologists call this tendency loss aversion: a loss usually feels considerably more painful than an equal gain feels pleasant. - Researchers suggest that this bias may have helped our ancestors survive. For someone with little food in store, losing a week’s supply could be fatal, whereas finding an extra week’s supply was merely welcome. A brain that treated threats as urgent therefore had an advantage. The same instinct, however, can be unhelpful when the threat is a falling number on a screen. - In investing, loss aversion tends to show itself in two ways. First, people who check their accounts every day see many small drops, and each one stings. As a result, they are likely to become more cautious than their goals require. Second, some investors refuse to sell a poor investment because selling would make the loss feel real. They wait for the price to recover, even when there is little reason to expect it. - Although the bias cannot simply be switched off, it can be managed. One useful step is to decide on your time horizon, meaning how long you can leave the money untouched, before you invest anything. Another is to write down the conditions under which you would sell. Looking at your account less often also helps. None of these habits removes risk, but they make it less likely that a moment of fear will make the decision for you.

🆚 After your first draft: the same content written three ways写完第一稿再看：同一个内容的三种写法

Plain平实版

Mei wants to put all her bonus into one stock. Her coworker told her about it. I think this is a bad idea. One company can lose money very fast. Nobody can see the future. Her husband wants to keep the money in the bank. He is right to be careful. But the bank is not perfect either. Money in the bank can lose value over time. I think Mei should do both. She should keep some money in the bank for emergencies. She can put the rest in many different places. She should ask her coworker for more information. A friend's tip is not enough. She can buy a little of the stock too. But she must not put everything in one place.

B2B2 版

I think Mei should not put her whole bonus into the one stock her coworker recommended, although I understand why she is tempted. If the company does well, she could gain a lot, but a single company can fail for reasons nobody can predict. A better plan would be to keep part of the money in savings, so that she has a cushion for emergencies, and to put the rest into a spread of different investments. Her husband is right to worry about losing the money, but keeping everything in a savings account has its own cost, because the value of cash can slowly shrink over the years. I would also ask Mei why her coworker likes this stock. A friendly tip is not the same as careful research, and excitement is a poor guide when real money is involved. However, if she can afford to lose a small amount and truly believes in the company, she could put a little into it. The important thing is that one mistake should not harm her family's finances.

C1C1 版

On balance, Mei would be unwise to commit her entire bonus to the single stock her coworker has been praising, however persuasive the tip sounds. Should the company thrive, the reward could be considerable; but any one firm can stumble for reasons that no outsider can foresee, and a loss of that kind would be painful. Her husband's instinct to protect the money is therefore sound, though a savings account is hardly risk-free either, since inflation tends to erode idle cash over time. What I would suggest is a middle course: set aside a cushion for emergencies, then spread the remainder across several holdings, so that no single disappointment can do serious damage. Enthusiasm, which is easily mistaken for evidence, is a poor basis for a decision of this size, and Mei would do well to ask what her coworker's confidence actually rests on. Provided that she can absorb the loss, a modest stake in the recommended company is defensible. What matters is that her family's finances never hinge on one person's hunch.

What changes from one to the next每升一档换了什么

  • The plain answer states the risk in two short, separate sentences ('One company can lose money very fast. Nobody can see the future.'), while the B2 answer folds the same idea into a single reason clause: 'a single company can fail for reasons nobody can predict.'基础版用两句分开的短句表达风险（'One company can lose money very fast. Nobody can see the future.'）；B2 版把同一个意思压进一个原因从句：'a single company can fail for reasons nobody can predict.'
  • B2 states the point in a short main clause, while C1 uses the more formal noun 'Enthusiasm' with a non-restrictive relative clause ('which is easily mistaken for evidence') that packs in why feelings are unreliable.B2 用一个简短主句表达这个观点；C1 改用更正式的名词 'Enthusiasm'，并接一个非限制性定语从句 'which is easily mistaken for evidence'，把“情绪容易被误当成证据”这层意思压缩进去。
