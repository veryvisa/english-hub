👂 Thursday: listen first, then read周四：先听懂，再看文字

“Who Watches the Decision-Makers?” is a text you have not read yet.「谁来监督决策者？」：这一篇你还没读过。 Three steps: listen without the text, answer, say the main points. Then open the text to check and shadow it.三步：不看文字听，答题，说出要点；然后打开原文核对并跟读。

1 · Answer without looking不看文字答题

1 What is the main purpose of institutional oversight?

Oversight makes decisions open to examination and correction rather than guaranteeing perfect outcomes.监督使决策能够接受检查和纠正，而不是保证所有结果都完美。

2 Why should accountability not simply mean finding someone to blame?

The article explains that complex decisions involve multiple departments, objectives, and incomplete evidence.文章指出，复杂决策涉及多个部门、不同目标和不完整的证据。

2 · Say the main points说出要点

3 · Now look at the text再看原文

📄 Show the text显示原文

- Every institution needs some form of authority. A hospital must decide how to organise its services, a university must determine academic standards, and a public agency must allocate limited resources. Yet authority creates an important question: who checks whether those decisions are reasonable? The answer reveals much about an institution's character. An organisation that can make decisions but cannot explain them may possess power without earning public confidence. - Oversight provides one answer. Independent reviews, transparent records, professional standards, and complaint procedures can all help identify errors or misuse of authority. These mechanisms do not necessarily prevent every failure. Their purpose is to make decisions open to examination and correction. While oversight may slow down administrative work, it tends to improve the quality of decisions when reviewers have genuine independence and access to relevant information. - However, accountability should not be confused with the search for someone to blame. Complex decisions often involve several departments, competing objectives, and incomplete evidence. If every mistake produces a defensive reaction, employees may conceal problems rather than report them. A healthier system distinguishes between reasonable errors, poor judgement, and deliberate misconduct. It also asks whether institutional procedures contributed to the problem. - Public confidence is therefore not simply a matter of popularity. People may dislike a particular outcome but still accept it if the process was fair, understandable, and open to review. By and large, institutions earn legitimacy through repeated demonstrations of competence and restraint. Their credibility is likely to weaken when explanations are inconsistent or oversight exists only on paper. The real test is whether accountability changes behaviour, not whether an organisation publishes impressive statements about it.

🆚 After your first draft: the same content written three ways写完第一稿再看：同一个内容的三种写法

Plain平实版

I think members should vote on big decisions. The club belongs to the members, so they should decide about fees. Now the committee decides alone, and many people do not know why. This makes them angry. A vote is slower, and that is a problem. But the club is small, and one meeting a year is not hard. Members can also ask questions at the meeting. The committee can still decide small things, like the pool hours. I support the change, but only for big decisions.

B2B2 版

I support the members' demand, provided that it applies only to major decisions such as raising fees. The club exists for its members, so they should have the final say over anything that affects their wallets. At the moment the committee decides behind closed doors, and people often only hear about a change once it has already been made. That naturally creates suspicion, even when the committee has acted in good faith. A yearly vote would force the committee to explain its reasoning in public, which usually makes decisions better as well as more accepted. However, the committee members are right to worry about delays. If every small matter, such as changing the lesson timetable, needed a vote, nothing would ever get done and volunteers would probably give up. Although a yearly meeting costs time, it is a small price for a club where people trust one another. So my suggestion is simple: the committee should keep running daily matters, and the whole membership should decide the few choices that change the club's direction.

C1C1 版

On balance, I would back the members' demand, provided that it covers only major decisions such as fee increases. The club exists for its members, who are the ones paying, so it is hard to justify keeping the final say with a handful of volunteers. At present the committee decides behind closed doors, and members tend to learn of a change only after the fact, an arrangement that breeds suspicion even when the committee has acted in good faith. A yearly vote would oblige it to defend its reasoning in public, which generally improves decisions as well as acceptance of them. The committee's fear of delay is not unfounded, though. Were every minor matter, such as the lesson timetable, put to a vote, the volunteers who keep the club running would probably burn out and walk away. A yearly meeting is a modest cost, however, set against the trust it builds. My suggestion, then, is a clear division of labour: the committee runs day-to-day affairs, while the full membership decides the few questions that shape the club's direction.

What changes from one to the next每升一档换了什么

  • The plain 'but only for big decisions' becomes a conditional clause with 'provided that', which states the limit as a clear condition on the view.简单版的 “but only for big decisions” 在 B2 中变成了带 “provided that” 的条件从句，把限制范围明确表达为支持的前提。
  • 'Force ... to explain' becomes the more formal 'oblige it to defend', where 'defend' adds the idea that the committee must justify itself, not just describe its choices.B2 的 “force ... to explain” 在 C1 中变为更正式的 “oblige it to defend”，其中 “defend” 多了委员会必须为自己的理由辩护、而不只是说明的含义。
